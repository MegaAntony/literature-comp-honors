Fahrenheit 451
Fahrenheit 451 was published in 1953.
It is a novel of social criticism that warns against the dangers of suppressing thought through censorship.
It uses the conventions of science fiction to convey a message that “oppressive government, left unchecked, can do irreparable damage to society by limiting the creativity and freedom of its people.”

What is Social Criticism?
Social criticism analyzes (problematic) social structures, and aims at practical solutions by specific measures, radical reform, or even revolutionary change. 
Fahrenheit 451 is a social criticism that warns against the danger of suppressing thought through the censorship of books.



Historical Context

Fahrenheit 451 was developed in the years immediately following World War II. This was the beginning of the Atomic Age and the Cold War. 
Other influential social criticisms of the time include George Orwell’s 1984 and Animal Farm.

The 1950s
By mid-century, nearly 60% of Americans were members of the middle class.
Consumerism = Success
The electronics industry became the 5th largest industry. Television had a widespread impact in American homes.
The United States became an automobile culture.
The increase in prosperity after the war led to an increased passivity and conformity. Jobs were plentiful and the common saying of the time period was: “follow orders, and you will succeed.”

Themes: Censorship
Censorship is defined as the removal or withholding of information from the public by a controlling group or body.
Typically done by governments, religious and secular groups, corporations, or the mass media.

Censorship in the Novel
In the world of Fahrenheit 451, books are burned because they trigger thought and discontent, two things that are unwelcome in this “happiness oriented” society.
What’s unexpected about the censorship in Fahrenheit 451 is that it seems to have originated with the people, not the government.
"Notice how in almost every case, censorship wasn't started by an evil dictator in a dark room. It started because everyday people wanted comfort, safety, and entertainment. When we open Fahrenheit 451, you are going to meet a world where the firemen don't burn books because the government forced them to—they burn books because society asked them to." 

Theme: Ignorance vs. Knowledge
Is ignorance bliss, or do knowledge and learning provide true happiness?
In this world, firemen promote ignorance by destroying books—and with them—knowledge.

Theme: Technology-Deadening 
Human Experience
Technology in Bradbury’s 24th century is highly advanced and dominates society.

TV, radio broadcasts, fast cars and atomic weapons dominate people’s experiences—at the expense of things like nature, music and poetry.


Theme: Dystopian Society
Dystopian Societies: an imagined place or state in which everything is unpleasant or bad, typically a totalitarian or environmentally degraded society. 
Dystopian societies have propaganda, restriction, constant surveillance, fear of the outside world, dehumanization, conformity, and a utopian illusion. 
Fahrenheit 451 is a dystopian piece of literature. 

Utopian Societies: A place of ideal perfection. 
It is perfect in respect to laws, politics, customs and conditions. There are no problems like war, discrimination or oppression 


Why do authors write dystopias?
Written to explore social and political structures. 
They make a criticism about current trends, political issues, or problems in society.
These warn or advise their readers
Create or establish future consequences for present day affairs
Explore society’s lust for greed, power, and control.


CHARACTERISTICS OF DYSTOPIA
Propaganda is used to control the citizens of society.
Information, independent thought, and freedom are restricted.
A figurehead or concept is worshipped by the citizens of the society.
Citizens are perceived to be under constant surveillance. 
Citizens have a fear or hatred of the outside world.
Citizens live in a dehumanized state.
The natural world** is banished or distrusted.
Citizens conform to uniform expectations; individuality and dissent are bad.
The society is an illusion of a perfect, utopian world. 



**ADD TO NOTES:
Natural world can mean nature OR how regular society is

TYPES OF DYSTOPIAS
Corporate Control
One or more large companies control society. 
Example: Minority Report, The Circle
Technological Control
Society is controlled by robots or computers.
Example: Terminator, I, Robot, Matrix


Bureaucratic Control
Society is controlled by a mindless government.
Example: 1984, The Hunger Games
Philosophical / Religious Control 
Society is controlled by a philosophical or religious ideology, often enforced through a dictatorship or totalitarian government 
Example: Divergent, Handmaid’s Tale

 
 
 
 

A DYSTOPIAN PROTAGONIST
Often feels trapped and is struggling to escape
Questions the existing society and political systems
Believes or feels that something is terribly wrong with society in which he or she lives
Helps the audience or other characters recognize the negative aspects of the dystopian world through his or her perspective
