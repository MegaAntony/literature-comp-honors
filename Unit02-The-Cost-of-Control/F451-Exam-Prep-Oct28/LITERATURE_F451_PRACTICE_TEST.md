### Multiple Choice Answers
1. **B** — Montag initially views burning as an art form and a symphony of order/power, showing how deeply indoctrinated he is before meeting Clarisse.
2. **A** — "Python" and "venomous" carry sinister, Edenic/serpentine connotations, framing the fireman's hose as a monstrous destroyer of wisdom.
3. **B** — The statement is a **paradox** (seemingly contradictory statement revealing a deeper truth), creating an uncanny mood around a machine that mimics life without possessing a soul.
4. **B** — Repeating *"gently"* creates a disturbing juxtaposition between the Hound's quiet mechanical hum and its lethal purpose.
5. **B** — Beatty uses staccato, one-syllable words (*"Click, Pic, Look, Eye, Now, Flick"*) to imitate the frantic pace of mass media that spins the human mind like a "centrifuge."
6. **C** — Ms. Grote's core takeaway: censorship started from the bottom up—citizens wanted comfort, speed, and never to feel offended or inferior.
7. **B** — Sand = truth/knowledge; Sieve = Montag's mind unable to hold onto it amidst the sensory assault of the Denham's Dentifrice jingle.
8. **C** — Verbs like *"vomited"* and *"pounded into submission"* establish a visceral, violent, and suffocating tone toward commercial propaganda.
9. **C** — Allusion to **Icarus**, whose wax wings melted when he flew too close to the sun; Beatty mocks Montag for reaching above his station toward forbidden knowledge.
10. **B** — Granger notes that while both the Phoenix and mankind repeatedly incinerate themselves, mankind has **memory** (preserved through books/factories of mirrors) to avoid repeating the cycle.

### Exemplar Responses for Section 3 (Compare Your Answers!)

#### Exemplar for Prompt 1 (Duality of Fire):
> At the outset of *Fahrenheit 451*, Bradbury characterizes fire through predatory, violent imagery—a "great python spitting its venomous kerosene" to reduce human history to "charcoal ruins." This destructive flame mirrors Montag’s initial blindness as a state instrument who mistakes obliteration for pleasure. However, after Montag sheds his fireman identity in the cleansing river, he discovers Granger’s wilderness campfire and marvels that the flame is "not burning, it was warming." By shifting the connotation of fire from annihilation to shelter and communion, Bradbury illustrates Montag’s transformation from a destroyer of knowledge into a preserver of human connection.

#### Exemplar for Prompt 2 (DBQ Synthesis — *Fahrenheit 451* + *"Rain"* / *"Dover Beach"*):
> The censorship of literature is profoundly dangerous because stripping away complex ideas does not produce true happiness; instead, it hollows out human empathy and leaves citizens emotionally paralyzed. In *Fahrenheit 451*, Mildred drowns her consciousness in Seashell radios and parlor walls, yet her repressed despair surfaces when she overdoses on sleeping pills and when Mrs. Phelps weeps uncontrollably upon hearing Matthew Arnold’s *"Dover Beach"*—a poem exposing a world that seems like a "land of dreams" but possesses "neither joy, nor love, nor light." Billy Collins’s poem *"Rain"* captures this exact psychological devastation: in a world where "there were no more books," citizens do not celebrate their newfound simplicity, but instead are found "curling up... quietly in corners and doorways." While Bradbury reveals how society masks its spiritual emptiness behind deafening electronic noise, Collins exposes the silent helplessness that remains once the noise stops—proving that without literature to help us process grief, humanity is reduced to frightened, mute isolation.
